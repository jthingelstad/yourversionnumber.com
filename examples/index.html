Eight examples

One person up to eight, every one of them a real page you can open.

The point of putting everyone on a single page is the comparison: whose major version is about to roll over, who shares a birthday with whom, how far apart two people really are once you stop counting in years.

  • Tim Berners-Lee

    One person, one number. He gave the web its addresses; this is his in three parts.

    1 person · zen

  • The Williams sisters

    Fifteen months apart, and you can read the gap straight off the minor version.

    2 people · polaroid

  • The Harry Potter three

    Cast as eleven-year-olds in 2001. All three are well into their fourth major release now.

    3 people · tarot

  • The Obamas

    What the site was built for: a household on one page, parents and children on the same scale.

    4 people · fridge

  • Radiohead

    Same school, same band since 1985, and still inside a four-year spread.

    5 people · nixie

  • The original six

    Two of them share a birthday seventeen years apart, so their patch numbers move in lockstep forever.

    6 people · memphis

  • BTS

    Seven people born inside five years. The minor versions bunch up; the patches scatter.

    7 people · departures

  • Eight language creators

    The people who gave us MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH in the first place, rendered in it.

    8 people · terminal

Every birthday here is public record; dates verified on 2026-09-09. Everyone listed is living — a version number that keeps incrementing for someone who has died is the wrong artefact to publish.

A joke by Jamie Thingelstad from 2018 that turned out to be useful. Source on GitHub.

Counting your age this way was David Hussman’s idea. He died in August 2018, six months after the post. This is kept in his memory.
